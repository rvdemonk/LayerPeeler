How it works

Three steps, one human, no dashboard to learn.

01

Send your mascot

One image — PNG or JPG, transparent background preferred but not required — plus the emotes you want. Email or DM. No account, no upload flow, no onboarding.

02

We generate and gate

Each emote is generated several times over and machine-checked: loop seam, colour drift, motion jank, on-model identity. Takes that fail are re-rolled, not shipped. You never see the rejects — that filtering is the actual product.

03

Delivered in 48 hours

A zip of .json and .lottie files, a private preview page like this gallery, and the four lines of code that drop them into your app. Not instant — async, and stated plainly.

FAQ

The technical questions, answered straight.

Why are the files ~1 MB when Lottie is supposed to be tiny?

Because these aren't vector Lotties. Lottie is a container as well as a vector format, and what's inside these is a sequence of 121 WebP frames with alpha, wrapped in the Lottie JSON schema so it plays in the players you already ship.

That buys the styles — painterly shading, gradient fills, organic motion no bezier rig produces — and it costs bytes: 0.7 to 2 MB gzipped instead of 50–300 KB. It is a real trade, not a temporary limitation. If your bundle can't spend a megabyte, this isn't the right tool.

Which players are verified?

lottie-web — verified; use the canvas renderer (121 image layers is a lot of DOM for the SVG renderer). lottie-ios 4.6.1 — verified in the simulator: it renders the frame-sequence format and decodes the embedded WebP assets, with per-frame output essentially indistinguishable from a lossless control, and first paint around 300 ms.

Not yet tested: Android's lottie-android, React Native, and older iOS versions (WebP decoding leans on the system decoder, so iOS 15/16 needs its own check). If you need one of those, say so before you pay and I'll test it against your file rather than guess.

What exactly do I receive?

Per emote: a .json (plain Lottie, everything embedded, no external asset folder to wire up) and a .lottie (dotLottie, ~25% smaller on disk). Plus a preview page like this gallery, and a snippet for whichever player you're using.

Can I get them smaller?

Yes, with a stated cost. There's a size ladder: 512 px at 24 fps is the default because it's the smallest setting that still looks premium. 256 px roughly quarters the bytes and reads as slightly soft at hero size but is near-native for a chat-sticker-sized element. Shorter loops cut proportionally. Dropping below 24 fps was tried and rejected — it reads as a flipbook. Ask for a rung and you'll get it.

Why not just ship a GIF or an MP4?

GIF caps at 256 colours with one-bit transparency — a soft-edged mascot gets a jagged halo over any background that isn't the one it was matted onto. MP4 has no alpha at all, so it can't sit over your UI. These files keep a full feathered alpha channel and composite over anything, which is what an in-app emote actually needs.

What styles work best?

Characters with a clear silhouette and a readable palette: flat vector mascots, 3D-render-style characters, hand-drawn and painterly illustration, soft-gradient blobs. What struggles: very fine detail and small text, which lose definition at 512 px, and anything whose appeal depends on razor-crisp geometric edges.

Send the image before paying — a straight answer on whether your style holds up is free.
